Acute:Chronic Workload Ratio (ACWR)

Theoretical background and calculation methods

1. Introduction

The ACWR is an index used in sports science to quantify the relationship between recent training load (acute) and the load accumulated over a longer period (chronic). The ratio is used to monitor the injury risk associated with sudden changes in training load.

The model is based on the fitness-fatigue paradigm first proposed by Banister et al. (1) and later developed in the context of injury prevention by Gabbett (2) and Hulin et al. (3).

1.1. Originally proposed zones

The studies that popularised the ACWR (2, 4) proposed the following thresholds. They are presented here as the original proposal, not as established evidence: later studies have questioned both the ratio and these cut-offs (see section 5, "Limitations and criticisms").

  • ACWR < 0.8: zone proposed as "undertraining": the acute load is clearly lower than usual.
  • ACWR 0.8 - 1.3: zone proposed as the "sweet spot", in which the injury risk was suggested to be lower.
  • ACWR 1.3 - 1.5: zone proposed as a caution zone.
  • ACWR > 1.5: zone proposed as high risk: the acute load far exceeds the usual chronic load.

It is important to stress that the ACWR is a decision-support tool, not an absolute diagnostic value. It should always be interpreted in the context of the individual athlete, the sport and other internal and external load indicators (5).

2. Rolling Average - Coupled method

This was the first method proposed to calculate the ACWR, widely used in the pioneering work of Hulin et al. (3) and Blanch & Gabbett (4).

2.1. Definition

In the coupled method, the acute window is included in the chronic window:

$$\text{Acute load} = \frac{1}{N_a} \sum_{i=d-N_a+1}^{d} L_i$$

$$\text{Chronic load} = \frac{1}{N_c} \sum_{i=d-N_c+1}^{d} L_i$$

$$ACWR = \frac{\text{Acute load}}{\text{Chronic load}}$$

Where:

  • \(L_i\) = load on day \(i\) (in arbitrary units, AU)
  • \(d\) = current day
  • \(N_a\) = length of the acute window (typically 7 days)
  • \(N_c\) = length of the chronic window (typically 28 days)

2.2. Limitations

The main problem of this method is mathematical coupling: because the acute window is included in the chronic window, both variables share data, which creates a spurious correlation between them. This was shown by Lolli et al. (6), who found that coupling can artificially inflate the association between the ACWR and injury risk.

2.3. How the app shows the loads

The app (and the ACWR package) expresses acute and chronic loads as weekly-equivalent sums: the sum over the window multiplied by \(7 / N\). With the usual windows, the acute load is the sum of the last 7 days and the chronic load is the sum of the last 28 days divided by 4:

$$\text{Acute} = \frac{7}{N_a} \sum_{i=d-N_a+1}^{d} L_i \qquad \text{Chronic} = \frac{7}{N_c} \sum_{i=d-N_c+1}^{d} L_i$$

Because both loads are multiplied by 7, the factor cancels and the ACWR is identical to the one obtained with the daily means of the formulas above; only the scale in which the loads are shown changes (AU per 7 days instead of AU per day). Days without a session count as load 0. In the uncoupled method, the chronic load is the sum of days \(d-N_c+1\) to \(d-N_a\) multiplied by \(7 / (N_c - N_a)\). EWMA is shown on the scale of a daily load.

3. Rolling Average - Uncoupled method

To solve the problem of mathematical coupling, the uncoupled method was proposed, in which the acute window is excluded from the chronic window (6).

3.1. Definition

The acute load is calculated as in the coupled method:

$$\text{Acute load} = \frac{1}{N_a} \sum_{i=d-N_a+1}^{d} L_i$$

The chronic load is calculated excluding the days of the acute window:

$$\text{Chronic load} = \frac{1}{N_c - N_a} \sum_{i=d-N_c+1}^{d-N_a} L_i$$

$$ACWR = \frac{\text{Acute load}}{\text{Chronic load}}$$

3.2. Advantages

  • It removes the spurious correlation caused by mathematical coupling.
  • It gives a more independent estimate of the acute:chronic relationship.
  • It has been recommended as a preferable alternative to the coupled method (6).

3.3. Limitations

Like the coupled method, it still uses simple moving averages, so every day within each window has the same weight. It does not account for the decaying nature of fitness and fatigue effects over time (7, 8).

4. Exponentially Weighted Moving Average (EWMA)

The EWMA method was proposed by Williams et al. (8) as an alternative to moving averages for calculating acute and chronic loads. It is based on the exponentially weighted moving average, originally described by Hunter (9).

4.1. Definition

The EWMA for a given day is calculated as:

$$EWMA_{today} = L_{today} \times \lambda_a + (1 - \lambda_a) \times EWMA_{yesterday}$$

where the decay constant \(\lambda_a\) is defined as:

$$\lambda_a = \frac{2}{N + 1}$$

Two EWMA series are calculated:

  • \(EWMA_{acute}\): with \(N = N_a\) (typically 7 days) -> \(\lambda_a = 2/8 = 0.25\)
  • \(EWMA_{chronic}\): with \(N = N_c\) (typically 28 days) -> \(\lambda_c = 2/29 \approx 0.069\)

$$ACWR_{EWMA} = \frac{EWMA_{acute}}{EWMA_{chronic}}$$

4.2. Initialisation

The initial EWMA value (day 1) is set equal to the load of the first day:

$$EWMA_1 = L_1$$

4.3. Advantages

  • It gives more weight to the most recent loads, better reflecting the decaying nature of fitness and fatigue effects (7).
  • It is sensitive to how loads are accumulated, not only to the total.
  • It distinguishes between athletes who reach the same total load with different time distributions (8).
  • Murray et al. (10) showed that the EWMA provides a more sensitive indicator of injury likelihood than rolling averages.

4.4. Key example (Williams et al., 2017)

Williams et al. (8) showed that three hypothetical athletes with the same total load over 28 days and the same load over the last 7 days have an identical rolling-average ACWR (1.43) but different EWMA values (1.25, 1.41 and 1.55, respectively). This illustrates that the EWMA captures the time distribution of the load, whereas the rolling average only captures the mean.

5. Limitations and criticisms

The ACWR is calculated here as described in the literature, but the ratio has well-documented limitations that should be known before using it to make decisions:

  • Mathematical coupling. In the coupled method, the acute week is also part of the chronic load. This creates a spurious correlation between the two and reduces the between-athlete variability of the chronic load (6). Moreover, the coupled ratio can never exceed 4 (15). The uncoupled method removes the overlap, but not the problems below.
  • Ratio artefacts. The ACWR behaves as a rescaling of the acute load: similar associations with injury are obtained when the acute load is divided by fixed or random "chronic" loads, and the ratio adds no predictive value over the acute load alone (13). A ratio is not always an appropriate way to represent changes in activity, and EWMA values depend on the initial load in the first weeks (14).
  • No validated thresholds. The recommended thresholds (e.g. the "sweet spot" of 0.8-1.3 in section 1.1) are affected by these limitations and by problems such as discretisation and sparse data, which undermine their validity as cut-offs for injury risk (13, 14). This is why the app marks the zones as indicative.
  • No benefit in a randomised trial. In a cluster randomised trial of 34 elite youth football teams (18 intervention, 16 control) over a 10-month season, planning training with the ACWR did not reduce health problems: prevalence difference 1.8 percentage points (95% CI -4.1 to 7.7); relative risk 1.01 (95% CI 0.91 to 1.12) (16). The result refers to that specific intervention in youth footballers.
