Footballer Workload Footprint (FWF)

A generalised representation of training load

1. Introduction

The Footballer Workload Footprint (FWF) is a method proposed by Matas-Bustos et al. (1) that generalises the concept of training load using tools from integral and differential calculus. Unlike the classic ACWR, which reduces all the information to a single ratio between two fixed windows, the FWF produces a full set of features that capture the time dynamics of training.

The model starts by representing the training history as a discrete time matrix (the FWF matrix), where each row is a load variable and each column a training event. In this app, each column is a calendar day.

2. The FWF matrix

For an athlete with \(V\) load variables monitored over \(n\) training events, the FWF matrix is defined as:

$$FWF = \begin{pmatrix} x_1(e_1) & x_1(e_2) & \cdots & x_1(e_n) \\ x_2(e_1) & x_2(e_2) & \cdots & x_2(e_n) \\ \vdots & \vdots & \ddots & \vdots \\ x_V(e_1) & x_V(e_2) & \cdots & x_V(e_n) \end{pmatrix}$$

where \(x_v(e_j)\) is the value of variable \(v\) at event \(j\). With a single load variable, the matrix reduces to a row vector.

3. Cumulative variations (∑ᵀFWF)

Cumulative variations generalise the concept of acute load. For each variable \(v\), each day \(e_j\) and each window size \(\tau\), it is defined as:

$$AW_v(e_j, \tau) = \sum_{z=e_j-(\tau-1)}^{e_j} x_v(z)$$

That is, the sum of the load over the last \(\tau\) days. For \(\tau = 7\) this is exactly the traditional acute load (sum of 7 days). For \(\tau = 28\), it is the accumulated chronic load.

The ∑ᵀFWF matrix contains every window \(\tau \in \{1, 2, \ldots, 28\}\), giving 28 features per variable that capture load accumulation at different time scales.

4. Temporary variations (ΔᵀFWF)

Temporary variations generalise the concept of change in load. They are defined as the difference between the current load and the load \(\tau\) days earlier:

$$\Delta_v(e_j, \tau) = x_v(e_j) - x_v(e_j - \tau)$$

This expression follows from the telescoping property:

$$\Delta_v(e_j, \tau) = AW_v(e_j, \tau) - AW_v(e_{j-1}, \tau)$$

That is, the temporary variation is the difference between today's and yesterday's accumulated load for the same window \(\tau\). It captures the net change in load relative to \(\tau\) days earlier:

  • \(\Delta > 0\): today's load is higher than \(\tau\) days earlier (increase).
  • \(\Delta = 0\): the load is stable.
  • \(\Delta < 0\): today's load is lower than \(\tau\) days earlier (unloading).

5. Advantages over the classic ACWR

The FWF approach addresses several limitations of the conventional ACWR:

  • No ratio needed: It avoids the problems of division by zero and the mathematical coupling inherent in the coupled ACWR.
  • Multiple time scales: Instead of two fixed windows (7 and 28 days), it analyses 28 scales at once, capturing short-, medium- and long-term patterns.
  • Predictive ability (limited evidence): In the data of Matas-Bustos et al. (1), features derived from the FWF performed better than the ACWR (coupled, uncoupled and EWMA) in machine learning models for injury prediction. Those data come from a single team over one season and the result has not been validated externally: the FWF is a descriptive tool, not a validated injury predictor.
  • Multivariable: The method is designed to work with several load variables at once (sRPE, distance, high-intensity running, accelerations, etc.), although it also works with a single variable.
  • Mathematical basis: It rests on concepts from integral calculus (cumulative sums) and differential calculus (temporary variations), giving a formal generalisation of the acute:chronic paradigm.

6. Interpreting the heatmaps

6.1. ∑ᵀFWF heatmap

Each cell (day, \(\tau\)) shows the load accumulated over the last \(\tau\) days. Low \(\tau\) values (1-7) reflect recent load; high \(\tau\) values (21-28) reflect accumulated load. A marked gradient from left to right indicates a progressive build-up of load.

6.2. ΔᵀFWF heatmap

Each cell shows the net change relative to \(\tau\) days earlier. Red indicates increases, blue decreases and white stability. A pattern of intense red in the recent rows indicates load spikes that might be associated with a higher injury risk.
